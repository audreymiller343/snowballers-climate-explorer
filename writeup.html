How we made this

Write-up for Is Winter Disappearing Faster Than Summer Is Warming?

Motivation

The motivation behind our project was to investigate whether winter warms consistently with summer warming or whether there remain differences from region to region, emission regions, and time period. We chose to explore this because a single global warming number can hide important seasonal and regional differences. Visualizing this problem allows viewers to compare two related yet distinct questions: Which season has warmed more relative to its baseline? And which season is warming faster over a selected interval?

The title, “Is Winter Disappearing Faster Than Summer Is Warming?”, encourages comparison rather than claiming that the project measures winter’s duration or snowfall. Given that our data includes seasonal temperature projections, we state this as a limitation on the page. We chose to narrow our project to winter-versus-summer warming because earlier research navigated annual warming regions across scenarios. The resulting question is more focused and also allows us to compare across three geographic regions, two emissions scenarios, and different year ranges.

Design rationale

We chose a time-series chart because the question is about change over time. This main chart plots the winter and summer temperature anomalies by year on the same vertical scale. The line make following each season’s trajectory and comparing them at particular year or across the selected period easy. Initially, we considered a chart that showed only the winter minus the summer gap. However, it would have hidden the two underlying seasonal trends. The final view we landed on keeps both lines and uses shading between them to show which season has the larger anomaly.

We show anomalies rather than raw winter and summer temperatures. Given that winter is naturally colder than the summer, comparing their absolute temperatures wouldn’t have been enough to see which season warmed more. Instead, we had each season measured against its own 1995-2014 baseline. The chart then labels the values as warming since the baseline. This allows the viewers to compare the changes without confusing a normal seasonal temperature difference with a difference in warming.

We distinguish accumulated warming from warming rate. The distance between the lines shows the difference in their anomalies by year. It is not a rate. In order to answer “which season is warming faster,” the page separately calculates a straight-line trend for each season over the selected years and states the comparison in plain language. The trends use the same interval for both seasons, and the page requires at least 20 future years before presenting a rate comparison. We landed on this combination because the viewer is able to see both the evolving seasonal differences and the rate comparison rather than them as interchangeable.

We use several visual cues to identify the seasons. Winter is strategically blue, and summer is orange and drawn with a dashed line. The differing line styles provide a secondary distinction beyond color. The fill between the lines is a pale version of those colors to indicate which season has the larger anomaly, with a legend explaining the exact mapping. Historical values are lighter, and the divider marks the transition from the historical simulation to projections. These visual cues help the viewer interpret the lines and their context without excessively relying on color alone.

We made region, scenario, and time period interactive because the answer depends on those choices. Viewers can choose a region and switch between moderate and high emission scenarios while also adjusting for start and end years. The three suggested views include Arctic, Europe sector, and north America sector. These regions offer a quick entry point to different patterns such as winter pulling ahead, summer leadin, or similar rates given the selected time window. An earlier prototype odf ouse suggested experimentong with displaying both scenarios together and switching between anomaly and gap measures. The current page focuses on the main chart and one scenario at a time and retains direct controls and suggested examples

We included hover and keyboard inspection for year-by-year values. Hovering reveals the seasonal values and their difference for a specific year. Keyboard users can focus the chart and use the arrow keys to inspect years. This supplements the overall trend with exact yearly details. The page also includes reset behavior, a responsive layout, and explanatory text regarding how to the read te chart ad what the data can and cannot show.

Development process

How we split the work

Audrey and Saahithi handled the data processing and chart code: pulling the CMIP6 data, building the seasonal averages, and writing the D3 chart. Audrey and Sadhana worked on design and interaction, including the layout, the suggested views, the shading, and simplifying the page. Saahithi made the checkpoint video, and all three of us wrote the write-up together.

Time

We worked on the project over three days, about 24 hours in total split among the three of us.

What took the most time

The hardest part was making it easy for readers to reach a conclusion, because the data itself is complicated. “Which season has warmed more?” and “which season is warming faster?” sound the same but can have different answers for the same region, and our early versions showed both measures without making the difference clear. We went through several rounds of simplifying: cutting the chart down to two lines, adding the shading, writing the answer as a sentence, and adding the suggested views so readers start on a pattern worth noticing. We also spent a good amount of time early on deciding what question to ask. We started by comparing yearly warming across regions and emissions scenarios before narrowing it down to winter versus summer. The data processing also took longer than expected, especially handling winters that span two calendar years and giving each season its own baseline.
