Is Winter Disappearing Faster Than Summer Is Warming?

How do the rates of winter and summer warming differ across geographic regions, and how might these seasonal differences change under future emissions scenarios?

A note on the title: we use “disappearing” loosely. The chart tracks how much warmer each season gets, not how long winter lasts or how much snow falls.

How to read the chart

Winter is December–February and summer is June–August. Each season is compared with its own 1995–2014 average, so a winter value of +2 °C means that winter is two degrees warmer than winters used to be. It does not compare winter’s actual temperature with summer’s.

The shading between the lines shows which season has warmed more by that year: blue for winter, orange for summer. The sentence above the chart answers “faster” using warming rates: the slope of a straight line fit to each season over the years you select, in °C per decade. Neither measure tells us how long winter lasts.

Data and methods

Selected CMIP6 model · monthly near-surface air temperature (tas). Complete seasons weight months by their calendar days. Winter 1995 includes December 1994; winter 2015 joins historical December 2014 with each scenario’s January–February.

Areas use published grid-cell bounds with fractional overlap at region edges. North America (25–70°N, 170–50°W) and Europe (35–70°N, 10°W–40°E) are rectangular sectors including land and ocean, not continent masks. The Arctic is 66.5–90°N at all longitudes and overlaps parts of the sectors.

These conditional projections describe one model realization, without ensemble uncertainty or a significance test. Trends depend on the selected interval. The baseline is recent, not preindustrial.
